## Process overview

For the blockout stage, I focused on building the first playable version of **Volcano Bunker** based on my earlier topdown layout and planning work. The goal was not to make the map visually finished yet, but to test the overall layout, room scale, player path, and basic gameplay ideas.

At this stage, I focused on greybox geometry: floors, walls, ceilings, gates, and major room shapes. I avoided final lighting and detailed texture work so that I could concentrate on structure and gameplay first. The blockout helped me figure out whether the map’s spaces made sense before committing to the final theme and polish.

## Greybox / prototype approach

The blockout was made as a rough first pass of the level. I used simple geometry to establish the main spaces and the relationships between rooms. The main goal was to create a playable route through the map and see whether the player could understand the basic progression.

I treated the blockout as a testing version rather than a final art pass. This made it easier to change room sizes, revise paths, and adjust the pacing of the map without worrying too much about finished textures or lighting.

## Design vision

The design vision for **Volcano Bunker** was to create a single-player Quake map set inside an underground bunker built around lava. I wanted the level to combine dangerous environmental hazards with combat encounters and simple progression.

The core ideas were:

- A bunker-like structure with connected rooms and corridors
- Lava as both a visual theme and gameplay hazard
- A readable main path through the level
- Encounters that become more intense as the player progresses
- Gates and progression points that help structure the experience
- A final room or endpoint that makes the level feel complete

## Layout and progression

The blockout was organized around a basic critical path through the map. I wanted the player to move through a series of connected spaces rather than just separate rooms with no relationship to each other.

The planned progression was:

1. The player starts in the bunker and learns the basic direction of the map.
2. The player moves through early rooms and begins encountering enemies.
3. The level introduces lava as a hazard.
4. The player moves through more dangerous rooms with stronger combat.
5. Key or gate progression gives the map clearer structure.
6. The level builds toward a final encounter and exit.

This structure helped me think about the map as a sequence of gameplay beats rather than just architecture.

## Room and area ideas

### Starting area

The starting area was meant to introduce the bunker theme and give the player a clear first direction. I wanted this area to be readable and not immediately overwhelming, so the player could understand the space before the harder sections.

### Lava room

The lava room was one of the main ideas of the map. Even in blockout form, I wanted this area to stand out as a hazard-focused gameplay space. The goal was for the player to feel tension from moving near or across lava while still being able to read the path.

### Combat rooms

The combat rooms were planned as spaces where the player would need to move, aim, and manage resources. In the blockout stage, I focused on room size and basic enemy placement rather than final detailing. I wanted the rooms to have enough space for movement but still feel dangerous.

### Gate / key progression

I planned to use gates, keys, or locked doors to structure the level. This helped give the map a stronger sense of progression and prevented it from feeling like the player was simply walking through unrelated rooms.

### Final area

The final area was intended to give the map a clear ending. At the blockout stage, this was mainly about reserving a space for a stronger final encounter and an endpoint, even if the details would be developed later.

## Gameplay in the blockout

The assignment asked for at least one area with gameplay, so I focused on making sure the blockout was not just empty geometry. I began thinking about where enemies, hazards, and pickups would go.

The gameplay goals at this stage were:

- Test whether rooms were large enough for Quake movement
- Test whether the lava hazard made sense in the layout
- Start thinking about enemy placement
- Think about where health and ammo would support the player
- Make sure progression through the map was understandable

The blockout was still rough, but it gave me a foundation for later gameplay and iteration work.

## Design decisions

One important design decision was to focus on the layout before final visuals. This helped me avoid getting stuck polishing textures before the level worked. I wanted the map to be playable first, then improve the theme, lighting, and detail later.

Another design decision was to make the lava part of the gameplay instead of only decoration. This made the theme more connected to the player’s experience.

I also tried to think about Quake scale and movement. The spaces needed to be large enough for combat and navigation, but not so large that they felt empty or unfinished.

## Challenges

One challenge during blockout was figuring out how much of the planned map I could realistically complete. The assignment notes that if the full plan becomes too much, it is better to cut or revise areas so the map can be completed. I had to think about the map as something that could be finished and iterated on, not just an ideal version that was too large.

Another challenge was making the early layout readable. In greybox form, everything can look similar, so the room shapes and path structure needed to do more work before final lighting and textures were added.

## What I learned

This stage helped me understand why blockout is important. It is much easier to change the level while it is still simple. Building the map in blockout form helped me test the major spaces before committing to final lighting, textures, and details.

I also learned that gameplay needs to be considered early. Even a rough room should have a purpose, whether that purpose is combat, navigation, pacing, or introducing a hazard.



## Next steps after blockout

After this blockout stage, the next steps were to add more functional gameplay, including enemies, triggers, doors, keys, pickups, and a clearer ending. The blockout gave me the structure I needed to begin iterating on gameplay and eventually add lighting, textures, and final polish.
